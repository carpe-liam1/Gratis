Modernist Design: How Cultural Framework is Imposed

Written by: Liam Larwood

Abstract

Since the western world began imperializing other countries in the late 15th century, the means of imposition have extended far beyond just physical control and into the mental realm. Ultimately, it is the modernist principles and strategies developed in the early to mid 20th century which have remained the most powerful modes by which western standards of thought are imposed within design. Ideals of functionality, efficiency, and minimalism all conceptualized though a western-capitalist world-view have embedded themselves in the curriculum and standard of design globally. This is a flawed status quo as it often creates dissonance between the design engineered through this thinking and the cultural environments they exist in.

Additionally, the impositional aspect of these standards lend themselves to assimilating or eradicating the use of the other cultures and their design manifestations. If we are to properly acknowledge the powerful role which culture has in fostering and facilitating social connections, the loss or assimilation of it in contemporary society is better framed as a growing identity crisis. Modernism and its standardization seem to be both an embodiment as well as driving force behind this trend. Such a trend is one which can be best characterized as Antonio Gramsci’s concept of cultural hegemony. Through its uplifting of eurocentric values and adherence to a eurocentric social system, modernism and its influence works as a primary cultivator of western-dominated capitalist society.

Essay

The designs we use and see today have been imbued with characterizations which trace back much further than that of the design’s purpose. Consider a typical phone which you may be using right now. The minimal ornamentation is a common design aesthetic and its rectangular composition is quite normal as well. These choices in design have much much deeper roots than just the function and functionality of the design. They are predicated on the dominant culture surrounding them; A culture and imposed social order.

Often design is seen as just the means to solve problems or facilitate functions. A fork is a fork after all. However, is a fork just a fork if it replaces chopsticks at a Chinese restaurant. It now serves not just as a new tool for eating but also an alternative to another tool, representing a different culture altogether. This is a trivial example but demonstrates the ways by which the cultural background of objects, and more specifically designs, serve to represent more than just their function. Designs and their affirmation in our society exist as a testament to the cultures which they represent.

In contemporary society, it is a eurocentric framework which imposes itself through the majority of design today. Since the western world began imperializing other countries in the late 15th century, the means of imposition have extended far beyond just physical control and into the mental realm. This permeates even past stratified social perspectives. Often it was not enough for a colonized group to be subordinate. They must also internalize and emulate the worldview which the empire wanted to impose.

Antonio Gramsci outlined this kind of attack on the conceptual front in modern day with his outlining of the term “Cultural Hegemony” which can be roughly described as the domination of society by a ruling class through the manipulation of culture. The implications of this assign a great deal of weight to the role of design in our world. After all, design accentuates and even defines culture. The question to be asked is which culture will a design represent? Especially in a world saturated with media and an ever decreasing system of localized communities, the global scale of designs today adds even more influence to the messaging of designs and what they represent.

With the western world often positioned as a vanguard of influence in design, one can only imagine what the world would look like if our designs were not made with the western ideal in mind. Jun'ichirō Tanizaki writes about this in his essay In Praise of Shadows where he analyzes the means by which Japanese standards of medicine trace their aesthetics back to western ideals. He writes “Antiquated medical equipment does have its drawbacks; but had modern medicine been developed in Japan we probably would have devised facilities and equipment for the treatment of the sick that would somehow harmonize with Japanese Architecture. Here again we have to come off the loser for having borrowed”(Tanizaki 21).

Through this example, Tanizaki illustrates the inferiority which is imposed through a lack of cultural representation in design. When a design and its framework is built out of a distinct cultural perspective, a certain dissonance is created by the design when utilized in an environment which does not share the same cultural background. With modern medicine equipment and procedures which were created by eurocentric standards, their application in Japanese society brings an imposition of western culture. Tanizaki acknowledges this imposition as one which is ultimately negative; eliminating the potential for culturally harmonious developments within design.

It is a common feature of many designs today due to the eurocentric roots of design standards which permeate a majority of design today. Ultimately, it is the modernist principles and strategies developed in the early to mid 20th century which have remained the most powerful modes by which western standards of thought are imposed within design. It is important to note that these frameworks are not inherently immoral or rooted in hegemonic ideals, but instead have been used for such purposes through their frame as superior ideals. These design standards often replace or at the very least, assimilate design manifestations of other cultures to the dominant western culture. It is through this that modernist design principles and standards push and cultivate cultural hegemony.

It is important to first outline the premise of cultural hegemony and how it operates. This is necessary to properly designate standards which propel this state of society and to understand the detriments which they are in active support of. Cultural hegemony was developed as a term by Antonio Gramsci in his prison diaries, recorded during his imprisonment by the Italian fascist regime from 1929 to 1935. In these writings, Gramsci never actually created an exact definition for the term.

The closest of kind to this is his often-quoted characterization of hegemony as "the 'spontaneous' consent given by the great masses of the population to the general direction imposed on social life by the dominant fundamental group.(3)” Consent is an important term to consider as it is the vessel by which imposition is allowed to proceed. It is the mental framework given to justify or standardize a social outlook which defends and upholds the current social system. Instead of being a concrete statement, this consent is passive normalization. It is the process of making the dominant group’s (western) preferences look like the only logical reality. The means of obtaining this consent is where design becomes involved. Gramsci outlined that consent was developed through a control over the dominant culture. Culture is defined through a distinct standard of beliefs,, norms, sentiments, values, and prejudices that support and define the existing distribution of goods, the institutions that decide how this distribution occurs, and the permissible range of disagreement about those processes. These intangible elements are embedded into every manufactured physical and visual thing around us due to the simple fact that everything human-made is a reflection of its environment. The words we use, the customs we render as proper, the institutions we inevitably interact with, they all shape every individual’s actions. Every individual’s actions include every creation. Every creation is a design which represents a certain reaction to the life around it. To quote the Guyanese historian Walter Rodney “A culture is a total way of life.” If design is both a reflection and the face of life, then design is culture.

Applying this framework of culture manipulation to design as both a representation and driving force of culture illuminates the role which design standards and trends possess in pushing for or against the dominant culture. Contemporary design standards take on this position in a unique way. They possess a larger range of influence as global networks facilitate and expedite the process of dispersing ideas and ideology. Simultaneously, they face competition and distraction unparalleled by any point in history due to the saturation of these global networks. The influence of post-structuralist trends in art and creation which snowballed since its conception along with the emergence of design’s digital democratization has further muddied the idea of an ultimate authority or dogma in design. While worth noting as a contemporary social trend, this assessment of the current state fails to acknowledge the corporate standards which design frameworks are subjected to. Ideals of efficiency and functionality control design standards through their embedded goal of adhering to and operating within the western-capitalist scope of society. Stratification remains primarily through the form of institutions and the frameworks they’ve developed in contemporary design standards, the most prevalent of these being Modernism.

Modernism is quite an amorphous term as its application as a framework and standards is dispersed across many fields, ranging from anthropological perspectives to visual styles. Particularly in relation to design, modernism represents both a set of ideations developed in the early 1900’s as well as visual and physical standards which followed and were based upon these concepts developing well into the late 1960’s. Within these ideas was a certain idealism and rejection of cultural relativism.

It was through the war-torn, social-unrest-ridden early 1900’s, that intellectual examinations of aesthetics and standards of the time sought to “fix” the environment which surrounded them. It was an outcry for something which would prove better than the current social environment which was defined at the time by state and social conflicts. This was on various fronts, ranging from intellectual and political perspectives to modes of visual and physical creation. During this period, countless visual movements sought to disrupt and dismantle the traditional ways of expression as they characterized them as the tenets of this dysfunctional world around them. Movements including but not limited to Dadaism, Surrealism, Constructivism, and the Bauhaus all presented new methods and standards for creation. It is notable that these discussions were made primarily within the west and were characterized as such, designating these developments as elements of western culture.

Similarly, design foundations mirrored this intellectual search for an answer. Much like the futurist perspective of embracing industrialization and efficiency, modernist design stressed an emphasis and eventually, isolation of the immediate function(s) of a specific design and succinct conveyance of them. It was of their opinion that by focusing on these aspects of a design’s usage, conflict resolution and process-analysis of the highest level was achieved. Such a goal outlines the justification of modernist styles, methods, and standards which were developed following this period. What was not considered was the plurality of function across different cultures along with the larger cultural issues which would develop due to an overt imposition of this framework.

Modernism and its principals, over the span of the past century, have been embedded into the status quo of design standards and processes. Initially this was through an overt popularization of modernity as a new aesthetic framework throughout the early to mid 1900s. Following this period in the 70s-2000’s, there was a heavy exploration and critique of these principles which came alongside the rise of counterculture trends. This perspective gave way to various visual and design movements which conveyed these discussions through visuals which rejected the modernist style standards. This never fully was standardized however as the establishments and corporations, which continue to exercise control over most products and their industries, have internalized most modernist principles in their practices. We can see these practices everywhere. A recent study conducted by the EPC group found that 75% of Fortune 500 companies explicitly dictate the use of Sans-Serif typefaces for their primary corporate identities and brand logos. Sans Serif typefaces which rose to prominence as a product of modernist design are portrayed as a manifestation of modernist principle like that of minimal and function design. This comes alongside the standardization of other modernist elements like that of minimalism and grid-system layouts. Just consider the grid framework of every website you see or the minimal ornamentation on most appliances. This extends past products as well. Architecture across the world is continuously directed by modernist frameworks as well. This often stems from the curriculum taught in design courses worldwide. Another recent study tracking architectural education history points out that the global landscape of design studio pedagogy underwent a major shift in the mid-twentieth century when traditional Beaux-Arts methods were replaced by doctrines of the Modern Movement. The study concluded that foundational training at international universities was and is permanently anchored in the rigid structural and graphic principles pioneered by the German Bauhaus, a movement which was instrumental in the progression of modernist standards. (Salama & Burton, 2023). From the visuals we see to the structures which define our environments, the underlying rules of global design—from grid-based layouts to functional minimalism—are still dictated by Western European principles of the early 20th century.

Within modernism, there is an idea of superiority. It is the belief that certain elements of a design and its applications must be properly attended to and valued above others. This implies that there is an ultimate “perfect” way by which design should be engineered which can be applied to all designs regardless of their environment. This is how modernism acts as a mode for obtaining the consent which Gramsci describes. Through its popularization, modernist ideals standardized western definitions of ideas like functionality and efficiency along with their prioritization with the design process. These ideas are fundamentally western-capitalist ideals which is why their imposition as “ultimate” ideals serves to affirm and validate only a western-capitalist society. The fundamental issue found within modernism is not in its goals to optimize creation nor its actual aesthetics. It is that embedded within this goal, which was then imposed through its application and popularization, was the idea that distinct western notions of functions and representation are superior to that of other cultures.

Modernist design principals often fail to consider the plurality of culture and the social obligations found within each one. Look at the fluid famous modernist phrase “form must follow function”, and see in it, a dangerous simplification. The “function” of a design can be understood from many different angles. A single object does not merely serve a practical utility, but simultaneously acts as a vessel for cultural, personal sentiment, and social backgrounds, and many other external elements. Modernism in its conceptualization, found itself within western circles of discussion where critiques of power-dynamics and external cultural issues were not at the forefront of the goals being agreed upon. This stemmed from an isolated framework which found itself only reacting to western-centered issues. Issues like that of the geographic disputes between European countries and inadequate financial conditions of western workers. When seen as a specific reaction to these conditions, modernism can be viewed as a much more noble and appropriate perspective. When analyzed in retrospect, through the lens of a globalized world where the influences of this perspective extend far from its own cultural framework, modernism is better understood as an imposing reductive force.

We can see these contained elements of modernism particularly in the utopian idealism found in Le Corbusier’s Vers une architecture, where the "house as a machine for living"(Le Corbusier 27) became a physical manifestation of social equilibrium. With this perspective, Le Corbusier, who is considered by many to be a foundational figure in 20th-century modernism , fails to acknowledge the ambiguity of certain terms and concepts. While the “house as machine for living” may ring true as a general assumption, It can easily be designated as a place of worship or building of communal practices through the lens of other cultures. While various practices like these ones can fall under the vague umbrella term of “living”, the way they manifest in different cultures is not considered. Consider the minimalist concrete approach which Le Corbusier pioneered. Blank walls, clean edges, and cheap accessible materials emphasized the modernist assertion that functionality, efficiency and industrial standardization should be prioritized over unnecessary visual and structural elements. But what is unnecessary? And what is functionality or efficiency? These terms all find their true meaning in the context which they inhabit. The white walls and sleek materials which Le Corbusier used adhered to functionality and efficiency as they were defined in an early 1900s eurocentric social landscape. This style emerged because building-grade cement was invented in the 19th century and began to be used in construction in the early 20th century. Concrete was cheap, making it a good material during a time of scarcity. The value of this trait is given by its surroundings of an individualist industrializing environment. Would concrete be as optimal if it were applied within a culture where ancestral preservation is of a higher value than that of industrial speed, or a culture where structural degradation and repair offer a mode of employment and community facilitation? The goals of modernism as well as the means of obtaining them are fundamentally rooted in the worldview from which they emerged. This worldview is not applicable to every culture nor does it benefit the preservation of every culture.

The Great Mosque of Djenné is a fascinating example of design from an alternative culture. First created by Mansa Musa was first islamic ruler of Djenné in the 13th century, the temple now stands in its most recent form as a recreation from 1907 which was developed under Colonial rule. It is worth noting that historians have often labelled the overall design as more “neo-Sudanese,” a style that the French forced upon their West African colonies and posts. Despite this obvious social imposition, the reconstruction was headed by Ismaila Traore, a local master mason who had great familiarity with the structural technique of authentic Djenné architecture, and contains many elements which escape eurocentric ideals. At its core, the Great Mosque of Djenné represents the old, traditional construction of mosques in Africa. Perhaps most visually notable, the wooden posts which jut out from the walls of the temple are both decorative elements as well as structural tools which play a role in the preservation of the temple. The main materials used in its construction are these beams along with sun-baked earth bricks (called ferey), mud plaster. The mud plaster which naturally erodes each year, facilitates a yearly replastering. This event has become a festival (called the Crepissage de la Grand Mosquée) which is a part of the identity of the Djenné locals. The entire replastering festival is a time where the whole of the Djenné community gathers and participates. There are many distinguishable elements of this building and the western norms of architectural design. The wooden beams and their extension to exterior would likely be deemed unnecessary under a modernist perspective since these elements don’t serve any immediate function of the building. However, when understood through the framework of the culture, the beams offer other means of creating “functionality” . They have created an aesthetic of the building which creates a strong identity for the community. They also support the structure of the building and act as scaffolding platforms for the continuous replastering of the building. This continuous replastering which may not be structurally optimal is validated in a different cultural value which is community. The yearly upkeep helps facilitate collective effort where all members of the community work together for a common goal, thus helping to maintain the social fabric of the community.

If we relocate to the east of the Djenné Mosque in Niger, we see a more contemporary example of cultural consideration within design. The Hikma Complex which was completed in 2018 is a library and mosque in Dandaji, Niger which acts to respond to both the contemporary needs of the local community whilst also upholding and validating the cultural expression and framework of the region it is within. The structure’s exterior is composed of locally sourced materials like compressed earth blocks while limiting the use of concrete to structural elements such as columns and lintels. This choice is based upon the previous mosque’s tradition of being built with the local materials, which allows the structure itself to feel far more comforting and familiar to the local community who will use it. The interior of the mosque along with the library contains ornamental patterns both etched into the structure itself and on top of it which were designed by local masons and artisans which serve to integrate traditional techniques and promote local craftsmanship. This implementation of more labor intensive materials and visuals all operate to create a structure which interacts with its cultural environment in harmony.

Examples like these exist across the world, where local communities which have created designs around them adhere to their own cultural framework. Their performance of tending to their culture through design can be seen as a healthy standard for design worldwide.

When comparing this process to the USA’s sphere of influence within its immediate regions, particularly more urbanized western communities, one can see a similar application of culture except that the standards imposed do not meet or benefit the preservation of localized culture. The centralized base of the United States has an extensive influence upon its local communities and the design around them, particularly through the indoctrination of modernist western principals. This application is flawed since globalization along with the colonial displacement and disbursement of cultures have resulted in a western world which is fundamentally cosmopolitan, brimming with various cultures with communities and views which are engineered very differently. These different social groups all exist under the same western world. Despite this, we often see the principles of modernism implemented in the processes and designs which are standardized across all these communities. In this comparison between western designs and the actual communities which they inhabit, we see a perplexing juxtaposition between the pervasive individualism and plurality of identity in the contemporary age and the imposed totalizing rhetoric of modernist standards.

A more visual example of modernism can be seen in the graphic design style, Swiss Style, and its principals which often are taught in design curriculum world-wide. Swiss style has another name which is the International Typographic Style. In its conception, it was portrayed, as the name suggests, as a new style to be applied on an international scale. Its main characteristics fundamentally align with modernist thinking due to its having the same conceptual roots of attempting to create an ideal system to be applied across all designs. One of the most prominent notions of Swiss Style is objectivity and minimalism where "less is more," unnecessary elements are often stripped for clear communication. This is the same modernist framework which designated ornament and cultural visuals as being unnecessary. Perhaps the most potent of the principals of Swiss Style, across all of design, is the Euclidean grid system. The grid system is a framework of intersecting vertical and horizontal lines based on Euclidean geometric space, which uses coordinates to organize elements within consistent distances and angles. Again we see eurocentric standards being enforced with the euclidean geometry being treated as a superior model to that of other cultures and their visual systems. Euclidian geometry is one of many visual systems across many cultures.

Two notable examples of alternative visual systems include African fractals and Islamic Girih, both of which incorporate more circular and interactive structuring. Fractal geometry, particularly its usage in African culture, is a specific form of mathematical visual arrangements whereby a certain shape or figure is repeated either within or adjacent to itself. The practice has been used to structure many African urban layouts and often falls in line with cultural designations for distinct functions of the society. This system, instead of relying on parallel horizontal and vertical lines, makes use of the repeated application of a form to create a visual layout. Often these patterns provide their own sense of layout through the root form used, creating their own visual structuring which is not defined by a particular system like that of horizontal and vertical axes in euclidean geometry. Communities like that of the Ba-ila in Zambia and the Kotoko in Cameroon have used this type of mathematics to design their urban plans. The Kotoko people, native to a community in Cameroon, in particular have used this structure to create building complexes which utilize the self-similar scaling system. Ron Eglash, a professor who visited these settlements, highlighted in his book African Fractals: Modern Computing and Indigenous Design the socio-cultural customs which found their own manifestation in this structuring: “ They (Kotoko men) explained it terms of a combination of patrilocal household expansion–A man would like his son to live next to him and so we build by adding walls to the fathers house .” This self-similar structuring also aligns with the Guti which is a royal insignia which depicts a rectangle within a rectangle. This model is best understood in its parallels to the structuring of the buildings of the Kotoko which contain rooms which rise in formality and respect as one goes further into the complexes. Eglash writes “The fractal scaling of the architecture is not simply the result of unconscious social dynamics; it is a subject of abstract representation, and even a practical technique applied to social ranking”. By using this system of referential scaling and shaping, the social framework of these communities is adhered to by their architectural design.

To explore a, perhaps more fitting, graphic example of these systems in use, one can look at the textile designs used by Kente and Kuba groups from Ghana and the Democratic Republic of Congo regions respectively. The Bakuba or Kuba people frequently feature patterns in their clothes and textiles where lines or shapes branch or repeat within themselves. These patterns and their positioning may seem irregular or disorderly but they remain constant in their adherence to formulating from specific forms. The visual of these patterns going from orderly to less orderly is a common occurrence. Seen vividly in the raffia palm textiles, there is a distinct transformation in shapes where their form is distorted. Despite this, the structuring of them as iterations of a certain gives them a specific framework for their creation. The Kente take this style of self-similar scaling in a different direction where certain visual forms have been compressed or lengthened on their garments. The weavers call this “spreading” the pattern and it is done to harmonize with the way by which these garments, traditionally togas, are worn on their bodies

Unlike the static Swiss grid, these visual fractal arrangements create a dynamic visual field which isn’t structured by rigid axes. This can be seen as mirroring the polyrhythmic nature of African music and urban layouts (like that of the Kotoko and Ba-ila settlements). By prioritizing scaling and recursion, these designs achieve a "holistic clarity" that allows for both cultural alignment and intricate aesthetic detail which conveys this alignment. They depict non-Euclidean systems which are still functional and organized.

The Islamic Girih system is another alternative visual system which has been used as the framework for much of Islamic visuals and decorative practices, particularly in tile work. The Girih system operates by creating a framework of tesselation, where a pattern of shapes perfectly interlock across a plane. The system, which has been developed over centuries, specifically uses five "girih tiles" which are the decagon, the pentagon, the elongated hexagon, the bowtie, and the rhombus. Often, patterns or shapes are constructed within or across these tiles in a radial fashion, framing them in a sort of infinite loop due to the seamless interlocking nature of the tiles. These designs and their geometry contain a more nuanced and complex visual framework than that of the Euclidean grid as it is interwoven with Islam’s own historical exploration of mathematics. Their variations and applications create densely woven patterns which act in geometric harmony. The significance of this system in relation to Islam can best be understood through the Islamic concept of Tawhid which roughly translates to "oneness" or "making one." This foundational doctrine holds that God is indivisibly one, and by extension, all of creation stems from a single source and is fundamentally interconnected. The Girih system's ability to produce endless, non-repeating patterns from a single set of tiles reflects this concept. Through its harmonious, interconnected designs, the Girih system visually manifests the unity of existence. This linkage between cultural foundations and visual design applications is explored in the book Geometric Concepts in Islamic Art written by Ayşe Parman and Issam al-Said. The foreword of this text emphasizes this connection, stating “In the Islamic perspective, this method of deriving all the vital proportions of a building from the harmonious division of a circle is no more than a symbolic way of expressing Tawhid, which is the metaphysical doctrine of Divine Unity as the source and culmination of all diversity. It is not surprising, therefore, that Muslim artists should have explored all the geometric systems that depend upon the regular division of the circle.” The Girih System acts as a framework which is fundamentally derived from a distinct culture as its conception can be understood as a cultural manifestation. In it we see a plain application of design being created through a cultural framework and eventually represented. Islamic structure along with items and visuals are lined with these patterns as they represent the background of these items.

These visual schemas all reflect various social paradigms which surround their conception. The standards of Swiss Style and, in a broader sense, Modernism are no different. They are one of the many cultural manifestations which inhabit our world. To frame or impose these principles as universal or objective would be to reject the fundamental truth of the world which is that we are not all the same and do not come from the same environments. It is this exact positioning of modernist standards which makes it an agent of hegemony. The designation of modernist standards as a core part of most design curricula globally, as well as the overt portrayal of certain pieces of this movement as pinnacles of design, place modernist values as an incredibly imposing framework; One which has embedded itself into much of the design around us today. To revisit Gramsci’s exploration of cultural hegemony, Modernism and its standardization can be understood best in the present as a pervasive element of the imposition of Western Culture on a global scale. It is a strong and seamless element of culture today which reinforces the western-capitalist social system which is dominant today.

The reason that this contextualization of modernism is significant comes from the many socio-cultural problems which arise in present-day society. The western-capitalist social model is plagued with many inadequacies. To outline these issues would be to try to summarize an ongoing list which many have dedicated their lives to. For the sake of cohesion with the current topic and argument presented, a specific issue from these lists which demonstrates a distinct issue of culture expressions and customs will be analyzed. There is a growing trend of isolation within the world. A study authored by Thomas E. Fuller-Rowell, Samia Sultana, and Ichiro Kawach in 2025 found that global social isolation surged by 13.4% over a 16-year period, climbing from 19.2% in 2009 to 21.8% by 2024. Countless other studies and commentaries have pointed out this worrisome trend, highlighting the growing sense of alienation amongst people with their communities and identities. Another study published by the International Journal of Humanities and Social Sciences Review highlights how, alongside this trend, there has been a growing decrease in cultural expression, particularly in assimilation with western influences as a result of globalization. The study emphasizes “ On one hand, globalization facilitates cultural exchange and diversity, enriching individuals' perspectives and fostering tolerance. However, it also leads to a dilution of unique cultural identities as traditional practices give way to more homogeneous, globalized lifestyles.”. Both these studies reveal distinct trends in cultural and social identity across the world. The two may seem isolated until they are viewed with the understanding that cultural expression and facilitation are major pillars of social interaction in communities across the world. Across the world, culture and its many social manifestations have remained the backbone of much socialization. This remains especially true in contemporary society, as content and art-based communities are the basis for many social groups and gatherings today. Large-scale community data from the HEartS Survey published in Public Health, which analyzed the impact of cultural participation across thousands of adults, revealed that 82% of respondents explicitly link arts and cultural engagement directly to feelings of social connectedness. If we are to apply this powerful role which culture has in fostering and facilitating social connections, the loss or assimilation of it in contemporary society is better framed as a growing identity crisis. Modernism and its standardization seem to be both an embodiment as well as driving force behind this trend.

The way by which modernism and its tenets have been standardized across much of design (particularly corporate design which is increasingly coming to define much of the design which surrounds us) acts as a push towards making the design manifestations of other cultures adhere to the western standard. This is due to the fundamental trait of modernism which places the priorities of its conception’s environment, a western-capitalist mass-producing society, above that of any other culture. This prevents modernist products from ever truly performing in harmony within cultural environments where the values and principals do not exist parallel to that of a western-capitalist world. Due to the western-capitalist social model encompassing the entire planet and with it the plethora of cultures from humanity, this dysfunction operates in direct correlation with the growing trends of identity and social issues. Due to the dominance of the western influence on a global scale, modernist products and their standardization force other cultures’ design to adhere or be rendered as archaic in relation to western standards. Not only is Modernism founded on and propped up on a fallacy of universal application. With its current dominance in the field of design, it actively plays a role in cultural assimilation and the prevention of cultural expression.

Ultimately, Modernist design reflects the goals and considerations of only one western culture which, propelled by globalization, have snowballed into a domineering force in design across the world. They have standardized a western-capitalist framework as they adhere to the values of such a social system. It is in this standardization that modernism and its popularity have cultivated cultural hegemony on a global scale. As a product of these standards being imposed and influential in the urbanization and development of non-western states, the cultures of these regions and their design manifestation are often aestheticized or assimilated into the modern norm. Within the western sphere, this homogeneity of design framework has lent itself to creating a space where localized cultures find themselves less represented and in an environment of design which reflects imposed conformity of modernity. One can see a distinct linkage between this trend alongside the growing issues of socialization and identity in the contemporary age. As the world progresses forward, a new cultural framework for design which emphasizes the importance of plurality in cultural manifestations and expressions, can be understood as a more appropriate approach to be taken. As the influence of modernism has demonstrated a clear lack of cultural consideration, it should be established that the goal of a design should not be to address one specific goal or function. It should instead be to adequately reflect and respond to a lived cultural environment and not one which is imposed.
